The Mandela Effect? Why Do People Think Artificial Intelligence Is Something New?

Have you ever been certain about something, certain enough that you could bet on it, and then find out that you were completely wrong? Like you remember a movie dialogue, a certain action, a logo, or any celebrity's death years before they actually did. And surprisingly, everyone around you remembers it the same way you do, so it feels true. It feels solid. And then one day you find out the truth, and you sit there wondering how so many people could be so sure of something that was never real. Maybe, many do not even believe you...

Well... coming back to the topic, that strange feeling has a name, the infamous "Mandela Effect". It is named after a similar fact that thousands of people around the world were sure that Nelson Mandela had died in prison during his struggle against the apartheid regime in the late eighties. Many even remembered his funeral coverage. They remembered his speeches and words. Some of them even remembered reading about it in school. But the truth is, Mandela did not die in prison. He got released from that prison, became president of South Africa, and lived for many more years after that. This memory was shared by so many people that it almost felt like some sort of proof. But a shared memory, even by a million people, is still just a memory.

Now here is the funny thing, we are now doing exactly the same thing with Artificial Intelligence. Right now, all of us, or at least most of us, have this firm belief that AI is something brand new, that it just popped up like a trend and is taking over literally everything. It is something that just showed up out of nowhere. Or, with a childish expression, "One day the world was normal, and the next day a robot was writing poems and answering questions and making everyone nervous about their jobs."

But that is not the truth, not even close. And the reason why we all believe it is honestly fascinating, because it says a lot more about us than it does about those invisible machines.

Well... it is time to bring you guys back to the past, not a few years, not a few decades, let's go all the way back to the 1950s. There was a group of very smart people, mathematicians and scientists, who got together at a place called Dartmouth College in the summer of 1956. They sat around and talked about a wild idea. What if we could build a machine that could think? Not just calculate, because machines had been doing math for a long time already (Google it). Their idea was something that looked impossible. How can machines even think, reason, and solve problems the way a living creature does? They even came up with a name for it during those talks. They called it Artificial Intelligence.

But wait... how did computers look back then? The internet was yet to be discovered in the next 14 years. Bill Gates was a few months old, Sam Altman and Elon Musk were in liquid form... sorry.

So the next time someone tells you that AI is a recent invention, you can gently remind them that the term has been around for almost seventy years. The idea itself is even older, way older. People have been dreaming about these thinking machines for centuries. There were stories about mechanical servants and talking statues long before electricity, quick transportation, or any modern thing we know. There were inventors in the 1800s who built machines that could play music or write letters, and people back then were just as amazed and just as frightened as we are today, probably. There was even an automatic chess player called "The Mechanical Turk," created in the 1770s. Even though it was a kind of scam, we are talking about the idea, not the invention.

I guess, even though the idea existed and the research was happening, most of us never noticed any of it because it was boring, or maybe because it was hidden, or too little to get our attention. It was doing the dishes while nobody was watching.

Think about all the small things in your day that happen because of AI. Your emails get sorted, the junk filtered out before you even see them. Your phone guesses what word you are about to type. Your music app builds a playlist that somehow knows exactly what mood you are in. Your bank spots a strange purchase and sends you a text. Your maps app finds you a faster route home when there is traffic. None of that is magic. All of it is artificial intelligence, and all of it has been quietly working in the background for years. You just never called it that. You never thought about it at all. It was just there.

Then something changed. The AI came out from behind the curtain and started talking to us. That is actually what happened. It did not suddenly get that smart overnight. It had been getting smarter for a very long time, slowly, quietly, and one small improvement at a time. But now it is available to us, directly having conversations. You could type a question and get a real answer. You could ask it to write a poem and it would write one. From image editing to drawing from scratch, doing your coding for you, to even singing a song. That was new, something different, and this is what everyone remembers, because that is the moment we could finally see it.

It is like a person who has been living next door to you for years, but you have barely seen them around. You have waved at them but you never really talked. Then suddenly, one day you actually sit down and have a long conversation, and you walk away thinking, wow, I never knew they were so interesting. The person did not change, your awareness did. That is what happened with AI. The intelligence was always there, but this conversation was new.

There is another reason we all think AI is brand new, and this one is a little more uncomfortable to admit. We love a good story. We love to feel like we are living at some turning point, at the edge of history, right where the important stuff is happening. It gives us something to talk about. So when the chatbots and GPTs arrived, the headlines started rolling in, and we grabbed onto the narrative with both hands. AI is here. AI changes everything. Nothing will ever be the same. And honestly, some of that is true. But the framing, the idea that this all started recently, is where we get it wrong.

People have been saying the same things for a very long time. When the telephone was invented, people panicked, they said it would destroy real conversation. When TV came along, they said it would rot our brains and damage our eyes. When the internet showed up, plenty of smart people called it a fad. Every generation thinks it is standing at the edge of something huge, and every generation is partly right and partly wrong. The technology is real, the fear is real, but the feeling of being the first is usually an illusion.

So what does it actually mean, this whole thing? Why does it matter that we remember AI as new when it is really old?

It matters because when we think something is brand new, we treat it like a toy, we poke at it, play with it, and assume that nobody has thought about the hard questions yet. But people have been thinking about these questions for decades. The worries about AI taking jobs, being biased in decision making, who gets to control the technology and who gets left behind, none of that started last year. Those conversations have been happening in classrooms and research labs since before any of us was born. We are not the first ones to ask these questions. We are just the latest.

Final Words

This is the real Mandela Effect here. Not that the machines changed, but that we finally woke up and noticed them. For years they were walking along in the background, sorting our mail and finishing our sentences, and we paid them no attention. Then one day they spoke, and we acted like it was the first time we had ever heard a voice. It was not the first time. It was just the first time we listened.

And if that is not a Mandela Effect, I do not know what is.
